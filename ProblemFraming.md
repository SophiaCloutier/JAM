# Domain

Our domain consists of creative hobbyists in a city. This network is made of people with a casual interest in creative activities such as videography, drawing, scrapbooking, and embroidery. This excludes professional creatives such as recording musicians, gallery artists, and filmmakers. These stakeholders typically devote time to their craft because of an intrinsic passion and curiosity, and for many, artistic endeavors provide a sense of mindfulness, self-expression, and connection with others. The domain can be described as a community rather than a group of separate individuals because there is a sense of solidarity between those with an appreciation for the arts, and creative projects often require close collaboration and support. A play can’t show without a director, set designer, actors, writers, and dancers. A short film can’t be produced without lighting technicians, cinematographers, and editors. Networks form as creatives cluster and weave together to pursue their ambitions and support others in achieving their own. 

# Bad situations

Art communities in Boston are disconnected. People looking to explore new creative hobbies can struggle to find accessible, low-pressure opportunities to try new things and connect with others through shared creative interests. For beginners who do not already have friends interested in the same activities, finding an art community can feel intimidating, especially when the communities they encounter seem geared toward experienced artists.

For example, consider someone who wants to try drawing as a hobby but has no experience and no friends who draw. They look for local art groups and events, hoping to meet people they can learn alongside. However, the opportunities they will find in Boston are intended for people who already have skills or work to share. They are unsure whether beginners are welcome and worry that they will be expected to keep up with more experienced artists. Rather than attend alone and risk feeling out of place, they put off trying the activity.

When this happens repeatedly as they look for hobbies to explore, they spend time searching without finding a comfortable place to start. They miss opportunities to develop creative interests and build meaningful connections, and trying something new continues to feel intimidating.

# Evidence

Creative communities can be hard to find, even in a city with a thriving art scene. In [*Are you creative? Feeling lonely? Join this group art class*](https://medium.com/nyu-journalistic-inquiry/are-you-creative-feeling-lonely-join-this-group-art-class-403b8b0167fb), Neha Magesh reports on artists in New York City who experience loneliness and difficulty finding community. The article describes how the demands of creative work, earning money, and self-promotion can leave artists with little time or energy to build connections. This supports our concern that being surrounded by other creatives does not necessarily make it easy to find people to create with.

The article also describes Happy Medium, where participants make art together in a welcoming, judgment-free setting. Co-founder Taylor Carraway explains that a shared creative activity gives participants a common talking point, while attendee Cat Chi describes how learning and creating alongside others can encourage connection. These accounts support the value of making art together. 

For the value of these opportunities being perceived as low-pressure, we turn to illustrator and artist Charlie Collins. She offers a similar [firsthand account](https://creativelivesinprogress.com/articles/charlie-collins-advice-on-overcoming-creative-isolation), describing how loneliness can diminish her creativity and highlights how social anxiety makes networking events difficult to attend. She describes workshops and shared creative activities as more comfortable ways to meet other creatives. Although her experience is partly situated in a rural context, it reinforces the importance of finding a community that fits someone’s interests and comfort level.

Existing solutions also provide evidence that these connections are possible. Happy Medium and local spaces such as Still Life Studios already bring people together through creative activities, demonstrating that people are interested in and benefit from these opportunities. However, hundreds of people on Reddit still struggle to find art activities and communities ([here](https://www.reddit.com/r/ArtistLounge/comments/1m6v9xb/how_do_you_find_20_art_communities_these_days/) and [here](https://www.reddit.com/r/ArtistLounge/comments/13550yg/how_did_you_find_your_art_groupcommunity/)). Their existence qualifies our claim: creative communities are available, but people may still struggle to find an opportunity that fits their interests, location, and availability. This is especially relevant for beginners who may not already have connections within these communities. JAM aims to reduce that matching and coordination burden. 

# Workarounds and Comparables

Workarounds

Currently, people often rely on word of mouth, social media, or existing creative clubs to connect with their local art community. Those who are already involved in artistic spaces or openly share their creative interests may have more opportunities to meet like-minded people who can introduce them to events and collaborations. However, people who are new to a hobby or don't have these connections may struggle to find opportunities in the first place. 

In Boston, people can participate in creative clubs such as [Still Life Studios](https://stillifestudios.com/), [Craft Loft](http://www.craft-loft.com/) or [Coalition of Boston Filmmakers](https://www.facebook.com/groups/bostonfilmgroup/). These spaces offer opportunities to develop artistic skills and meet others with similar interests. However, depending on the organization, participation may involve scheduled classes, membership fees, or recurring commitments. While these arrangements work well for people who already know what they want to pursue, they can be less appealing to someone who wants to casually explore different hobbies. Committing time or money to a particular activity before knowing whether they enjoy it creates an additional barrier to participation.

Another common workaround is connecting through informal group chats and social media communities. You might meet someone at an event, or have a friend who has a friend, and they might know of artistic opportunities you could be interested in. This often takes the form of being added to a group chat or exchanging contact information. Other people try their luck by making Facebook announcements or exploring Reddit threads. However, reaching out to strangers or asking to join existing groups can be intimidating for beginners and hobbyists who are still building confidence in their creative interests. Furthermore, these networks only expose people to the opportunities they happen to hear about, leaving other potential activities and collaborators undiscovered. As a result, people may miss out on creative experiences and potential friendships simply because they aren't connected to the right people. 

Comparable Apps

[*Collabr*](https://www.collabrapp.com/) connects creatives in their local area through meetups, in-app chats, and video calls. While it helps people network, showcase their work, and build connections, it places much of the responsibility for initiating and organizing activities on users. Individuals must come up with their own ideas, create events, and coordinate meetups, which can discourage those who want to participate but don't know where to start. Additionally, its emphasis on professional networking, self-promotion, and potential monetization may make interactions feel more transactional than recreational. This can be less appealing to beginners or hobbyists who simply want to explore creative interests and meet others without the pressure of building a professional presence.

[*Clubs*](https://clubsapp.org/) helps people find collaborators for projects that can take place online, in person, or in a hybrid format. Users can propose project ideas, recruit collaborators with relevant skills, and communicate with their teams. However, this approach assumes that users already have a project in mind or are willing to commit to someone else's. For people who want to explore creative hobbies but don't know what to make, coming up with a project idea can become a barrier to participation. The platform also emphasizes recruiting collaborators and completing defined projects, which may be less suitable for spontaneous, low-pressure creative activities that don't require specific skills or long-term commitments. Even after finding collaborators, users must still coordinate when and where to meet, leaving much of the organizational effort unresolved.

# Solution sketch

JAM is a low-stakes app that connects beginners in Boston who want to explore creative hobbies and make art with others. Users create a profile by listing their creative skills, interests, location preferences, and availability. Based on these preferences, JAM automatically generates local creative events and invites users to participate in one event at a time, giving them the option to accept or decline without any ongoing commitment.

For activities that require complementary skills, such as forming a band or hosting a jam session, JAM brings together participants who can fill the necessary roles. For more casual activities, such as drawing or scrapbooking, it matches people based on shared interests, availability, and location preferences.

JAM lifts the matching and scheduling burden off users, reducing the pressure to approach potential collaborators individually or coordinate an event themselves. Each event is a standalone opportunity to make art together, develop creative skills, and meet people with similar interests; whether participants keep in touch afterward is up to them. This makes it easier for beginners to step outside their comfort zones and explore creative interests they might otherwise miss out on due to limited connections or confidence. 

# Ethical analysis

## Insight \#1

Dimension: Pervasiveness  
Prompt: Widespread Use  
Feature: Event generation  
Observation: Below a certain number of users, the pool of people sharing skills, interests and availability is too small.   
Consequence: Some users may be matched with the same people over and over again, or never matched at all. As adoption grows, the pool widens matches become more varied.   
Design response: Use a history-aware randomized match to avoid repeat pairings and prioritize users not invited recently.

## Insight \#2

Dimension: Stakeholders  
Prompt: Indirect stakeholders  
Feature: Event locations  
Observation: The app places events in public cafes around Cambridge, but per out initial design, cafe staff and regular customers never opted into JAM.  
Consequence: If many events map to the same cafe, groups can fill tables for hours, crowd out paying customers, and frustrate cafe staff.  
Design response: Cap events per venue per day, aim for a large enough list of cafes so that a truly random algorithm avoids clashes, and perhaps introduce an opt-in option for cafe owners.

## Insight \#3

Dimension: Time  
Prompt: Long-term use  
Feature: No in-app messaging  
Observation: JAM has no way to message or follow other attendees, so connections must continue in person.  
Consequence: Event attendees who would like to pursue a friendship are forced to favor in-person interactions and exchange information on their own terms. Also, no one will be able to contact them uninvited.  
Design response: Keep messaging out, and end each event with an in-app prompt encouraging attendees to swap contact details in person for shy beginners who might need a push.

## Insight \#4

Dimension: Stakeholders   
Prompt: Direct Stakeholders  
Feature: RSVP  
Observation: Users accept an invitation to a small group event, but someone may accept and then not attend.  
Consequence: Other participants waste time, and the event may not be as fun as it was intended when proposed.  
Design response: Require a small deposit when accepting, refunded upon attendance or cancellation before a clearly stated deadline. Provide deposit waivers and emergency exceptions via admin contact so accountability does not exclude users with limited funds or penalize unavoidable absences. 

## Insight \#5

Dimension: Values  
Prompt: Value Tension  
Feature: Automatic event matching  
Observation: Matching happens automatically according to shared skills, interests, availability, and location.  
Consequence: A beginner matched with advanced people may feel out of place, and an advanced person with beginners may feel unchallenged. Strict matching avoids this but limits learning, creating tension between proficiency and inclusion.  
Design response: Split profiles into skills (good at) and interests (want to learn). Default to beginner-friendly events; experience-required ones are opt-in.
